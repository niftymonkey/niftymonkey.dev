<Position id="what-this-is" title="What this is">

The [dossier](/notebook/adopting-ai-evidence) holds the evidence. The [guide](/notebook/adopting-ai-engineer) holds my positions: what I believe an engineer should do as AI is adopted where they work. This entry holds the order. The guide's practice section is a bunch of habits across three modes of work, and nobody picks those up all at once; honestly, nobody starts with habits at all. Everybody starts basically the same way: by playing with the thing. So this entry is built on how you'll actually arrive: play first, and let what happens while you're playing tell you which habit you're ready for.

</Position>

<Position id="play" title="First, just use the thing">

You're going to spend your first stretch playing: throwing tasks at it, poking at its limits, getting a feel. Good. That's not a detour from the fundamentals; it's where they come from.

Most of this entry stages one mode, producing, because that's where your first months live: the habits your first weeks hand you on their own, then two more that only make sense with some mileage behind you. The other two modes don't run on that clock, so they wait at the end as situations: read them once, and come back when one finds you. None of this is new advice. It's the guide's practices in the order your own use will ask for them, each with the concrete way to do it. The links here are few and they're all tools; the backing lives where it always did.

</Position>

<Position id="first-weeks" title="What the first weeks hand you">

<Arrives>Arrives on its own, in your first weeks</Arrives>

A few experiences are near-universal in those weeks, and each one is teaching a habit. Find the one that sounds like your week; the lesson is attached.

<Moment habit="Routing" title="It nails something you expected to take an hour, then confidently botches something trivial.">

For a while my whole model was that it's good at some things and bad at others, and I couldn't have told you which was which. It's more learnable than that: the tool is task-shaped. It's strong on the bounded and checkable, and unreliable wherever the real context lives in your head.

<Move>Before handing anything over, ask the guide's two questions: is it bounded and well-specified, and can a machine tell me it worked? Migrations, test scaffolding, boilerplate, and conversions go; work that needs your context stays.</Move>

</Moment>

<Moment habit="Review" title="Work that looked finished turns out subtly wrong later.">

Mine put a real bug on the main branch: the work had been reviewed, the checks were green, and I'd missed a piece of functionality I would have caught by reading the final version or exercising it by hand. My first instinct was to start reading everything from then on, the way you would for a brand-new junior. But if that's what the tool costs, describing the work and then re-inspecting all of it, I'm slower than if I'd typed it myself, and eventually I'd stop reaching for it. So I split the job instead: whatever I can name, I hand to the machine as an instruction or a check it runs. My reading is for what's left over, and that bug was exactly that kind. No check was going to see it; only a person looking at what actually got built.

<Move>Machine checks first, then read for the tells this code actually has: duplicated code where reuse existed, brute-force fixes that dodge the root cause, tests that assert nothing, any weakening of CI (that last one is a hard stop). When you do catch something, ask whether an instruction or a check could have caught it for you. Usually one could, and writing it down means you never have to make that catch twice.</Move>

<Read>[GitHub's ten-minute review protocol for agent PRs](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/agent-pull-requests-are-everywhere-heres-how-to-review-them/) is the checklist version of the same hunt.</Read>

</Moment>

<Moment habit="Plan first" title="You keep interrupting it mid-flight to redirect.">

Mine sounded like "no, not like that," again and again, until I saw the pattern: it kept solving problems in ways I wouldn't, because I'd never said how I wanted them solved. Coding standards, implementation patterns, a preference for industry-standard solutions over clever bespoke ones: all of it was in my head. The argument about the approach happens either way; it's cheaper before the code exists.

<Move>Flip the direction of the conversation: have it interview you, one question at a time, until it can write the plan; approve the plan; let it build against the plan. The exception: if you can describe the diff in one sentence, skip the ceremony.</Move>

<Read>A lot of how I actually work I learned from Matt Pocock. His [seven-phases write-up](https://www.aihero.dev/my-7-phases-of-ai-development) is the whole arc, idea through shipped, and his [open-source skills repo](https://github.com/mattpocock/Skills) runs it.</Read>

</Moment>

<Moment habit="Verification" title="You spend an afternoon verifying work you delegated to save time.">

This one is my present tense, not my past. On a current project the functionality lands almost embarrassingly fast, and then I live in the loop: almost there, but the fix exposed another issue, which exposed another. Delegation without a check the machine can run isn't delegation; you've handed off the typing and kept the verification.

<Move>Name the command that proves it worked before you hand work over: the test suite, the typecheck, a build that fails loudly. Tell the agent to run it as it goes, and make it show evidence rather than assert success. Commit checkpoints, so a wrong turn costs a revert instead of an argument.</Move>

<Read>[Anthropic's best-practices doc](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices) is worth ten minutes when you set this up; it's where the evidence-over-assertion rule comes from.</Read>

</Moment>

You'll know this layer is holding when routing stops being a decision you make and starts being a reflex you notice yourself having. That takes a few weeks of real work, not a weekend of experiments.

</Position>

<Position id="signals" title="When the signals show up">

<Arrives>Arrives with mileage, once there's something to notice</Arrives>

Keep working that way and the work keeps teaching. Two habits are still to come, and they're held back on purpose: in your first weeks there's nothing to measure yet and not enough delegation to steer. Each arrives with its own signal. Don't add the habit before its signal shows up; unprompted habits are the ones that don't stick.

<Moment habit="Measurement" title="You hear yourself telling someone the tool is saving you hours, and realize you're quoting a feeling, not a number.">

I've said something very similar myself, numerous times. The trouble is that the feeling is the wrong instrument: in the randomized trial [the dossier leans on](/notebook/adopting-ai-evidence), developers believed AI had sped them up while measurement showed the work taking longer. The number has since been revised; the gap between belief and measurement is the part that survives.

<Move>Start keeping receipts: two or three cheap ones, checked monthly. How much delegated work survived to merge without rework, how often it got reverted, where the hours actually went.</Move>

</Moment>

<Moment habit="Steering" title="The same wrong turn shows up twice.">

The second time the same mistake comes back, the thing that breaks isn't the session; it's my trust in it. From there, "done" is just a claim, and I make it show its work. Honestly, there are still occasions when I patch longer than I should before acting on that shift. That's the steering lesson: work the seams (the plan, the first wrong turn, the final judgment) instead of hovering over every line.

<Move>After two failed corrections on the same problem, stop patching: reset with a better prompt that carries what you learned, or take the work back yourself. Starting over beats wrestling more often than it feels like it should.</Move>

<Read>[The best practitioner writing on steering](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/13-role-of-developer-skills.html) is where the stopping rule comes from: end the session when you feel overwhelmed by what's going on.</Read>

</Moment>

</Position>

<Position id="situations" title="Two situations to come back for">

<Arrives>Arrives by circumstance, on no calendar</Arrives>

Everything above is the guide's producing mode. These two are the other modes, learning and leading, and they're not later stages; they're situations. They arrive by circumstance, they recur at every level of seniority (a principal engineer learning a new domain is, for that domain, starting over), and one might even be live for you today. Read them once so you'll recognize them; come back when one finds you.

<Moment habit="Learning" title="The first time you're learning something new with AI in hand.">

The settings flip. Producing optimizes for output; learning runs on the opposite rules: in the one randomized trial we have, the highest scorers used AI heavily, for concepts, and wrote their own code, while the group that wrote code but outsourced the debugging landed near the bottom.

<Move>Ask it concepts, tradeoffs, why this instead of that; then write the code yourself. Attempt first, with a real timebox, before asking. Keep some reps with no assistance at all. And if your tool has a [learning mode](https://claude.com/plugins/learning-output-style) that makes you write the load-bearing lines yourself, turn it on.</Move>

</Moment>

<Moment habit="Leading" title="The first time you try to bring another engineer along.">

The on-ramp stops being something you climb and becomes something you point at. Doing this with friends has taught me that it splits people. One of mine is getting it: seeing the value, learning, visibly improving. Another decided early that he wants nothing to do with it, and no argument I've made has moved him. I've stopped trying; the moments above have to happen to you. That's the half of this I've lived, the peer half. The lead half, where another engineer's growth is actually your job, I haven't; what follows there is the best of what I've read, not what I've done.

<Move>Smaller than evangelism: help someone pick one bounded task, and get out of the way. If another engineer's growth actually is your job, the tools are team-shaped: get the working agreements written down (what gets delegated by default, what review AI output gets before merge, what it must never touch) and revisit them quarterly. Keep a weekly go-wrong log of where AI-generated code caused friction. And guard the training budget: the bounded tasks agents do best are the ones juniors have always learned on, and software is still an apprenticeship industry.</Move>

</Moment>
